# Cost-of-Living Orientation

Switzerland is genuinely one of the most expensive countries in the world, but "expensive" doesn't have to mean "unaffordable." Locals have a whole set of habits for keeping costs down without sacrificing quality, and visitors can borrow most of them. This guide covers where to shop, where to sleep, and how to travel without paying tourist prices.

---

## Why It Feels Expensive

Switzerland's high prices mostly come down to labor costs. Wages are high across the board, and that gets reflected in everything from a coffee to a haircut. Groceries and self-catering are comparatively far more reasonable than eating out, which is the single biggest lever visitors and new residents have for controlling costs.

---

## Where to Shop: Grocery Stores by Price Tier

Switzerland has a clear hierarchy of supermarket chains, and knowing it will save you real money over time.

**The two giants**
- **Migros** — the largest supermarket chain in the country, and generally the cheaper of the two big names. Look for the **M-Budget** label, Migros's no-frills in-house brand for the best prices on staples.
- **Coop** — the second-largest chain, slightly pricier than Migros on average, but with its own budget line, **Coop Prix Garantie**, worth seeking out.

**The discounters**
- **Denner** — Switzerland's third-largest chain and a genuine discount supermarket, owned by Migros since 2007. A reliable choice if price is the priority.
- **Lidl** and **Aldi** — both entered the Swiss market in the 2000s and remain the cheapest options for a basic weekly shop, especially for packaged goods and household staples.

**Rule of thumb:** for fresh produce and quality, Migros or Coop; for the cheapest possible basket, Denner, Lidl, or Aldi. Mixing and matching (staples from the discounters, specific items from Migros/Coop) is completely normal and how most residents actually shop.

## Where to Sleep: Cheaper Alternatives to Hotels

Hotels in Switzerland are consistently one of the priciest line items on a trip. Two well-established, high-quality alternatives can cut that cost dramatically without a drop in comfort.

### Swiss Youth Hostels
Don't let the name put you off — Switzerland's official youth hostel network (part of Hostelling International) is a genuinely excellent budget option for travelers of any age, not just backpackers. Locations range from major cities (Zurich, Geneva, Basel, Bern, Lucerne) to alpine resort towns (Zermatt, St. Moritz, Interlaken, Grindelwald) and are often surprisingly close to trailheads. Facilities are typically clean, modern, and include private rooms as well as dorms — a solid option for families, not just solo travelers.

### Camping
Switzerland has an extensive, well-maintained network of campsites — including many in genuinely spectacular alpine or lakeside settings — that make camping a legitimate way to holiday in the country, not just a fallback for tight budgets. Facilities at Swiss campsites tend to be excellent: hot showers, on-site restaurants, and family-friendly amenities are standard even at modestly priced sites. Many sites are run through the TCS (Touring Club Switzerland) network or are independently owned; both tend to be well-reviewed and reliably maintained.

Camping is worth particularly highlighting for a hiking-focused audience: many sites sit directly on trail networks or near cable car stations, making them a practical (and scenic) base rather than just a cheap one.

## Getting Around Without Overpaying

A few transport habits can meaningfully cut your travel costs (see the [Getting Around](#) section for the full pass breakdown):

- **Book ahead for Supersaver fares** — SBB releases a limited number of discounted, train-specific tickets in advance. Less flexible (you're locked to that train), but significantly cheaper than a standard fare.
- **Saver Day Passes** — a discounted, date-specific unlimited-travel pass, best booked as early as possible (up to 60 days ahead) for the lowest price.
- **Off-peak travel** — where flexible, traveling outside commuter peak hours can unlock better fares.

## Other Everyday Savings

- **No tipping required** — service is included by law in restaurant and café prices. Rounding up is a polite gesture, not an obligation — you won't offend anyone either way.
- **Tap water is excellent and free** — Switzerland has some of the best drinking water in the world; there's no need to buy bottled water, and most restaurants will give you a glass of tap water on request (this isn't always automatic — you may need to ask).
- **Lunch menus (Tagesmenü / plat du jour)** — many restaurants, especially at lunch on weekdays, offer a significantly discounted set menu compared to their evening à la carte prices. This is often the best way to eat out affordably.
- **Picnicking** — with public grills, lakeside spots, and picnic areas everywhere, buying food from a supermarket and eating outdoors is a normal, sometimes preferred, way to eat in Switzerland — not just a budget compromise.

---

## The Bottom Line

You can absolutely holiday — or live — well in Switzerland on a modest budget. The trick isn't avoiding the expensive things entirely; it's being deliberate about where you spend (a big alpine excursion, a special dinner) and where you save (accommodation, everyday groceries, daily transport). Locals do exactly this, and visitors can too.
