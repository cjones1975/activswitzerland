# Getting Around

Switzerland's public transport network is one of the best in the world and once you understand a few basics, it's remarkably easy to use. This guide covers how to get here from the airport, which pass makes sense for your situation, and one of the country's most charming (and useful) quirks: the yellow PostBus.

---

## Arriving: Switzerland's 3 Main Airports

Switzerland has three main international airports, and all three connect directly to the national rail network, so your journey into the country and your first train ride often happen in the same building.

**Zurich Airport (ZRH)**
The train station sits directly beneath the terminal. Trains into Zürich Hauptbahnhof (the main station) run every few minutes, and from there the entire country is within easy reach.

**Geneva Airport (GVA)**
Also has its own train station inside the terminal, with frequent trains into Genève-Cornavin, the city's main station. The ride takes about six minutes.

**Basel Airport (BSL/EuroAirport)**
The exception: Basel's airport doesn't have an on-site train station. Note that this airport is shared with France and sits just across the border, so check which arrivals hall you're in. A short bus or tram connects you to Basel SBB, the main Swiss station.

**Tip:** Zurich and Geneva airports each offer a free public transport ticket for the journey into the city center, look for the ticket machine near baggage claim. Terms can change, so treat this as "ask at the station" rather than a guarantee.

## Which Pass Do You Actually Need?

Switzerland's public transport runs on a small family of passes and cards. Which ones you need and whether you need a SwissPass account at all, depends mainly on how long you're staying.

### Don't mix these two up: SwissPass vs. Swiss Travel Pass
The names are almost identical, but they're different things:

- **SwissPass** is an *account*, not a ticket, the card (physical or digital) that residents' longer-term travel products (Half-Fare Card, GA Travelcard, regional passes) attach to.
- **Swiss Travel Pass** is a *product for visitors*, a standalone multi-day ticket that doesn't require a SwissPass account at all.

If you're only here short-term, you can ignore SwissPass entirely, it only comes into play once you're buying a longer-term pass as a resident.

### If you're here short-term (visiting, or here for a few months)

No SwissPass account needed for either of these:

- **Swiss Travel Pass** — Unlimited travel across trains, buses, and boats for a set number of days, plus free entry to several hundred museums. Designed specifically for visitors rather than residents, and sold in consecutive-day or flexi-day formats.
- **Half-Fare Card (1-month)** — If you won't be traveling every single day, this is usually the better value: you pay half price per journey rather than a flat unlimited fee. (Note: even the short-term Half-Fare Card is loaded onto a SwissPass, but setup is quick and doesn't require residency.)

### If you're moving here or staying long-term

These all live on a SwissPass account, which you'll set up once when you buy your first one:

- **Half-Fare Card (1-year, renewable)** — The default choice for most residents. Halves the price of nearly all train, bus, and boat tickets across the country.
- **GA Travelcard (Generalabonnement)** — Unlimited nationwide travel for a flat annual fee. Worth it only once your travel volume passes a certain point — SBB has an online calculator to help you work out your own break-even point rather than relying on a rule of thumb.
- **Regional and cantonal passes** — Many cities and cantons offer their own add-on passes (for example, Geneva and Zurich both have local transport networks that layer on top of the Half-Fare Card). These vary a lot by region, so check with your local transport authority once you know where you'll be based.

## The Yellow PostBus

If you spend any time hiking in Switzerland, you'll meet the **PostBus** (PostAuto in German, CarPostal in French, AutoPostale in Italian), the cheerful yellow buses that reach the villages and trailheads no train line ever will. For hikers especially, this is one of the most useful things to understand early: some of the best trailheads in the country are *only* reachable by PostBus.

### A bit of history

The PostBus has deeper roots than most newcomers expect:

- The story starts in **1849**, with horse-drawn mail coaches, "diligences", carrying both post and passengers across the country.
- The first **motorized** PostBus service began in **1906**, running between Bern and Detligen.
- Through the 1920s, PostBus opened up some of the most dramatic alpine routes in Europe: the **Simplon Pass** in 1919, followed by the **Grimsel, Furka, San Bernardino, and Oberalp passes** in 1921.
- The last horse-drawn mail coach in the country ran the Avers–Juf route until **1961**, a reminder of just how recently this transition happened.
- In 1923, mountain-route buses were fitted with a distinctive **three-tone post horn**. To this day, on certain mountain roads marked with a yellow posthorn sign on a blue background, PostBuses have legal right of way over other traffic, worth knowing if you're driving in the Alps.
- PostBus became its own subsidiary of Swiss Post in **February 2005**, formally separating passenger transport from mail delivery.

### Practical info

- The network today covers hundreds of routes across the country, carrying well over 100 million passengers a year and reaching some of the most remote valleys in Switzerland.
- PostBus runs on the same ticketing system as trains. Your SwissPass, Half-Fare Card, or GA Travelcard all work exactly the same way here. No separate tickets or logic to learn.
- For route planning, go directly to postauto.ch/en.
